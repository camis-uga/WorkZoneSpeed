GDOT Research Project RP 25-13 · University of Georgia

Evaluating Speed Reduction Techniques in Construction Zones to Improve Safety

An evidence-based look at which speed reduction methods actually slow drivers down in Georgia work zones, so that workers and the traveling public get home safely.

Sponsored by GDOT & FHWA 6 active work zones Field radar + video data
Overview

Why work zone speeds matter

Work zones narrow lanes, shift traffic and put people a few feet from moving vehicles. Speeding in these conditions raises both the chance and the severity of crashes, especially rear-end collisions near queues.

This project measures how drivers actually respond to the traffic control used in Georgia construction zones. We record vehicle speeds before, within and after active GDOT work zones, compare sites with different layouts and countermeasures, and use the evidence to recommend speed reduction techniques for GDOT.

01 · Literature review

What the research says

We reviewed published evaluations of work zone speed reduction methods. Traditional traffic control devices typically lower speeds by only a few mph, and how well they work depends on the site. GDOT reviewers recommended looking more closely at emerging, data-driven techniques.

Traditional

Temporary transverse rumble strips

About 1–2 mph. They alert drivers through sound and vibration, and are especially effective for trucks.

Traditional

Dynamic speed feedback signs

About 3–6 mph. Speed trailers show each driver their own speed.

Traditional

Portable changeable message signs

About 1–4 mph for general messages, and up to 7 mph when the message is triggered by the approaching vehicle.

Traditional

Variable speed limits

Results depend on the site. They use sensors for corridor-wide monitoring, smooth traffic and reduce crashes.

Traditional

Advisory speed signs

Little added reduction where advance warning signs are already present.

Traditional

Portable traffic control signals

Wireless and programmable. They resolve conflicts at lane closures more than they lower speeds.

Emerging

Work zone queue warning systems

Sensors detect slowing traffic and report it to the traffic management center, which warns approaching drivers on upstream message signs. These are being tested in several states.

Emerging

Connected vehicle (I2V / V2I)

Roadside units share signal, work zone and hazard data with vehicles. For example, Ann Arbor's smart intersection project runs at more than 20 intersections.

Emerging

Automated speed enforcement

Camera-based enforcement in work zones. It was the technology State DOT respondents named most often as promising.

02 · State DOT survey

What practitioners told us

We surveyed 30 work zone professionals from 21 State DOTs, including Nevada, North Carolina, Washington and Florida. Respondents included chief construction engineers, work zone engineers and traffic operations engineers. Most work in mixed environments (74%) and on high-speed highways (70%).

What respondents need

Higher-resolution, real-time traffic data to make emerging tools work. Other ideas included automated flaggers to reduce worker exposure, and intelligent systems that communicate directly with travelers.

Rated highest for effectiveness: law enforcement, queue warning systems, dynamic speed feedback signs and temporary rumble strips. Rumble strips are the only one of these that is also low cost. Advisory speed signs rated lowest for effectiveness.

03 · Field data collection

Six active GDOT work zones

The sites cover an urban freeway bridge replacement, rural interstate resurfacing, a new roundabout, an arterial widening and a multi-lane closure, so that the same speed measures can be compared across very different settings.

    03 · Collection & analysis

    Measuring speed through the work zone

    At each site we set up portable roadside cameras, with radar for ground-truth speeds, at positions before, within and after the work zone. Every vehicle is tracked through each camera view, and its speed is estimated from calibrated camera geometry and checked against radar.

    Typical setup. Pole-mounted cameras cover the approach, the work zone and the downstream segment. Radar mounted beside a camera view gives ground-truth speeds for calibration, at night as well as by day.

    Record

    Cameras on portable masts capture every vehicle at each position. Radar beside a camera view logs ground-truth speeds and counts.

    Calibrate & track

    Camera views are calibrated to road geometry. Vehicles are detected and tracked, and their speeds are checked against radar, with an average error of 0.3–0.6 mph.

    Measure

    For each position we compute mean, median and 85th-percentile speeds, speed compliance, and flow rate per lane.

    Compare

    We compare positions, sites, times of day and countermeasures to see what holds speeds down.

    Key takeaways so far

    What worked
    • Police presence. With police vehicles upstream and inside the SR 141 closure, every vehicle observed stayed under the limit.
    • Lane closure with dense traffic. Dropping three lanes to one held speeds to about 17–23 mph at the start of and inside the zone.
    • Complex geometry. The roundabout at a curved T-junction had the highest compliance of the uncongested sites.
    Needs improvement
    • Long, straight and rural segments, where drivers return to normal speeds mid-zone.
    • Long-standing work zones that regular drivers have grown used to.
    • Open layouts and downhill approaches, such as the low-elevation bridge segment.
    Recommendations
    • Spread enforcement before and along the zone, not only at the entry.
    • Add mid-zone measures on long straight segments, such as speed feedback signs or rumble strips.
    • Refresh signs and messages in long-duration work zones.
